6 There are also stock market bubbles. In a normal market, investors buy stock in a

believe the value of the company's assets will increase. Sometimes, though, a “herd

up a thermometer to levels that prove unrealistic. Eventually, it becomes clear that

swift arop. When this happens to too many companies in aggregate, it is called a stock
market crash.

7 A striking example of a stock market bubble is the “dot-com” bubble. The buzz about the
economic possibilities of the Internet encouraged investors to fund the creation of many
dot-com companies - too many, it turned out. For several years, dozens of entrepreneurs
sought to duplicate for themselves the results of those that had come before. Many
investors envisaged wealth for any business with a website that could advertise on TV
or billboards, even if its actual services were ambiguous. Instead, on March 10, 2000，
the dot-com boom reached its peak when the stock index hit 5,132.52. Over the next two
and a half years, the index dropped to as low as 1,108.49. Very few companies bucked
the trend. Most had blundered into awful financial difficulties, selling off their assets to
healthier companies.
